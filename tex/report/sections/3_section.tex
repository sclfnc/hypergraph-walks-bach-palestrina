\section{Network Representation}
\label{sec:representation}
Each corpus becomes a weighted hypergraph whose hyperedges are the configurations of notes on which the style has rules, and a random walk on it writes new music.
\subsection{Modeling}

A random walk moves on a graph that exists before the walk starts, so we build one weighted hypergraph per style from its corpus.

\subsubsection{Nodes}
A node is a voice with a value: a transposed pitch (Section~\ref{sec:transformations}) or a rest. The voice is the type of its part: Soprano, Alto, Tenor, Bass and, in Palestrina, Baritone; Tenor~1 and Tenor~2 share the nodes of their type. The voice stays in the node because the rules of counterpoint hold between two parts \cite{fux1725} and because voices cross.

\subsubsection{Hyperedges}
A hyperedge is an ordered tuple: the node of each of $v$ voices in each of $s$ consecutive cells of the grid, and $v \times s$ is its shape (Figure~\ref{fig:shapes}). Battiston et al. define a hyperedge as a set of nodes \cite{battiston2020}; we keep the order, as Hadjeres et al. do for their quadrilateral \cite{hadjeres2016}, because a set cannot tell a held note from a single note, or a move from the move back. Each hyperedge belongs to a class, which holds its pitches: the scale degree of the lowest note of its first cell and the intervals of the other notes from it. The degree is the interval, modulo 12, from C, the tonic after the transposition; Witten and Conklin call it the interval from the referent, a referent that acts as ``an unchanging drone bass'' \cite{witten1993}. The intervals give one class in every octave. Below three voices the class also names the voice types, because the line and the counterpoint belong to named parts. From three voices up the hyperedge is a sonority, and the same chord sung by other parts is the same class. The other details of a window go on a ticket attached to its class, each with its own counts: whether each voice attacks or holds its note, the parts that sing it, the position in the bar and, in Bach, the fermata.

\subsubsection{Shapes in the literature}
Table~\ref{tab:shapes} lists the shapes of the model, what each one is in the music, and the models that use a shape of the same size. Shape $2 \times 2$ is the smallest on which Fux's rules of motion are defined: they forbid reaching a perfect consonance by similar motion, and so forbid parallel fifths and octaves \cite{fux1725}. Pairs of notes miss these rules: in the pairwise model of Hadjeres et al., 61.5\% of the generated $2 \times 2$ configurations occur in the corpus, yet consecutive fifths and octaves are 2 to 10 times more frequent than in the corpus \cite{hadjeres2016}.

\subsubsection{From the corpus to the hypergraph}
The grid turns each voice of a file into a sequence of nodes, one per cell, and every window of $v$ voices and $s$ cells belongs to one class; no window spans a passage that the corpus skips. Each window carries its fold and its ticket: for each voice and cell an attack flag that says whether the voice holds its note or attacks a new one, a distinction that Allan and Williams \cite{allan2004} and Whorley et al. \cite{whorley2013} also draw; the parts; the position in the bar of its first cell; and, in Bach, the fermata of each cell.

\subsubsection{Weights}
The weight of a hyperedge is the support of its class, as in the mining of association rules \cite{agrawal1993}: the share of the windows of its shape that belong to the class, each window counted $1/k$ times for a file with $k$ versions (Section~\ref{sec:transformations}). A class that never occurs has no hyperedge. A detail on the ticket has a probability given its class: its count with the class, plus an escape to its share in the whole shape, by method C as Witten and Bell describe it \cite{witten1991}. A class seen $n$ times with $r$ distinct details leaves $r/(n+r)$ to that share, so a class seen rarely, and always in the same way, still leaves room for a detail it never showed. In Bach, the sonorities are counted apart for each pattern of fermatas, because the melody brings its fermatas and the walk never chooses them.

\subsubsection{The walk}
The walk moves one walker per voice: at each cell all walkers move together, and a move is more likely when the hyperedges it completes have a higher support. The walk chooses the pitches by the supports and then the details by the tickets. Each detail is decided in one place, so that no choice counts twice: whether a voice holds its note or sings it again on the ticket of its line, and the parts and the fermata, which the piece gives, on the ticket of the sonority. How the supports of overlapping hyperedges combine is still open.

\subsubsection{Which shapes we keep}
The model uses three shapes, one for each kind of rule that the style follows: $1 \times 2$ for the line, $2 \times 2$ for the motion of two voices, and $4 \times 1$ for the sonority (Table~\ref{tab:shapes}). Five more shapes enter only the ablation, which adds them one at a time to test whether the style needs them: $1 \times 4$, $1 \times 8$, $2 \times 4$, $4 \times 2$ and $4 \times 4$. Their lengths come from the cross-entropy of every shape up to four voices and four cells, of one voice up to sixteen cells and of two voices up to eight. For each shape, the counts of nine folds predict the pitches of the last cell of every window of the tenth fold from the cells before it, in bits per voice per cell, as Witten and Conklin \cite{witten1993} and Whorley et al. \cite{whorley2013} compare viewpoints and orders. When a context has never led to a class, the same escape passes $r/(n+r)$ of the context's probability to the context one cell shorter. A single voice still predicts better at eight cells than at four, so $1 \times 8$ enters. Two voices predict worse at eight cells than at four in Bach, 1.18 against 1.04 bits, and best at six cells in Palestrina; we keep $2 \times 4$ in both, so that the two styles have the same shapes. Our lengths count cells, while the literature counts notes, slices or beats: one beat of a Bach line holds about one note. We found no threshold on coverage in the literature, so coverage, the share of held-out windows whose class occurs in the other folds, only describes how sparse a shape is, as in Sears et al. \cite{sears2017}.

\subsection{Transformations}
\label{sec:transformations}

\subsubsection{Transposition}
We move every file to the white keys, so that pieces in different keys add to the same counts. A Bach chorale moves so that its tonic falls on C if its key is major and on A if it is minor, as in BachBot \cite{liang2017}. In Palestrina, a movement in \emph{cantus mollis} moves down a fourth, into \emph{cantus durus}. The shift, the number of semitones that a file moves, is always the nearest one, at most six up or down, so that the voices stay within their range. The transposition is global: a passage that modulates keeps the degrees of the home key, as in Hadjeres et al. \cite{hadjeres2016}, BachBot \cite{liang2017} and BacHMMachine \cite{zhu2021}. Models that follow modulations add the local key as a separate variable: DeepBach as metadata estimated bar by bar \cite{hadjeres2017}, BacHMMachine as a hidden state learned from annotated chorales \cite{zhu2021}. We leave the local key out, because it would give the walk a harmonic analysis and Palestrina's masses have none. The degree is a point of reference, not a constraint: the nodes hold every pitch the corpus uses, so a passage in G keeps its F$\sharp$ and its own classes, measured from C. What the global key loses is sharing. The leading tone that rises to the tonic is one class in C, from B to C, and another in G, from F$\sharp$ to G, each with its own share of the counts; a key that the corpus rarely reaches has few classes. Major and minor chorales are counted together; Hadjeres et al. and Allan and Williams model them apart \cite{hadjeres2016, allan2004}, and counting them apart is an ablation.

\subsubsection{Grid}
The model reads time on a grid of equal cells, each a quarter of a beat long: a sixteenth note in Bach and an eighth note in Palestrina (Table~\ref{tab:grid}). A note that lasts several cells fills each of them, attacked in the first and held in the rest (Figure~\ref{fig:shapes:grid}). Each cell holds the note that sounds at its end, whereas DeepBach \cite{hadjeres2017} takes the note that sounds at its start. The two rules differ only on the nine ornaments of the Bach soprano, where two thirty-second notes share one cell. On all nine, the end rule keeps a note of the chord and drops a note outside it, so it keeps the harmony and loses only the ornament.

\subsubsection{Repeats and versions}
A passage under a repeat sign counts once, as written. Some files write the repeated passage in full instead; when the two occurrences are identical, we skip the second one. Bach has 26 such repeats. Two occurrences that differ by at most 7.4\% of their notes are versions of one passage, and so are two files that differ that little. Each version enters with the weight $1/k$, where $k$ is the number of versions, so that the notes they share count once and each variant counts $1/k$. This is the sequence weighting of Henikoff and Henikoff \cite{henikoff1994}. The threshold of 7.4\% sits in a gap in the data: the closest pair of files above it differs by 10.7\%. With these weights, the corpus that the model counts has 359 rows for 352 Bach chorales, which weigh as 333 pieces, and 1,181 rows for 1,179 Palestrina movements, which weigh as 1,178. Together with the skipped repeats, the weights move the supports of Bach's shapes by 0.69 to 0.76 times as much as leaving out one fold does, and those of Palestrina's by less than a tenth as much.

\subsubsection{Interludes}
In ten of the chorales whose instrumental parts we removed (Section~\ref{sec:dataset}), the instruments also play passages of their own, during which all four voices are silent. We cut these interludes from the grid. The sequence breaks at each of them, as at a skipped repeat, so that no hyperedge spans an interlude and the walk does not learn a silence that Bach gave to the instruments. A rest of every voice anywhere else stays, because the composer wrote it. DeepBach excludes every chorale with instrumental parts \cite{hadjeres2017}. Our cut keeps the sung phrases of these chorales.
